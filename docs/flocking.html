Flocking

The animated simulation visible on the Home page models the emergent collective motion of bird flocks using the boids algorithm, a computational framework first described by Reynolds (1987). This page documents the theoretical foundations and mathematical formulation of that simulation, and surveys the broader scientific literature on collective motion — from the statistical-physics models that followed Reynolds to the empirical reconstruction of starling murmurations and the active-matter theory that connects them.

Background

Reynolds (1987) observed that the coordinated movement of animal aggregations — bird flocks, fish schools, ungulate herds — could not be explained by a centralized controller or a leader-follower hierarchy. Instead, such patterns emerge from purely local interactions: each individual responds only to its immediate neighbors, yet coherent global structure arises spontaneously. He proposed three steering behaviors, each operating within a perception radius \(r\), that together reproduce the qualitative dynamics of natural flocks.

Collective Motion: A Brief Review

Reynolds’s 1987 model was an engineering achievement before it was a scientific one — he wanted convincing computer-generated flocks for film, not a theory of animal behavior. The bridge to biology and physics was built over the following three decades, first by statistical physicists identifying a new class of phase transition, then by empirical biologists who had cameras fast enough to track real starlings in three dimensions.

Self-propelled particles and the order–disorder transition

The decisive reframing came with Vicsek et al. (1995), who stripped Reynolds’s rules down to a single interaction: at each timestep, a particle adopts the average heading of its metric neighbors, perturbed by noise. Despite this minimalism, the model exhibits a genuine phase transition. Defining the polarization order parameter

\[\phi \;=\; \frac{1}{N v_0}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{v}_i\right\|\]

the system passes from \(\phi \approx 1\) (collective alignment) to \(\phi \approx 0\) (disorder) as noise increases or density decreases. Because each particle consumes energy to move, time-reversal symmetry is broken and the system has no equilibrium counterpart — this is a non-equilibrium phase transition in a self-propelled particle (SPP) system. Whether the transition is continuous or weakly first-order was debated for over a decade and ultimately resolved as a discontinuous transition in the thermodynamic limit.

Interaction zones and collective states

Couzin et al. (2002) returned collective motion to a biological framework by formalizing three concentric interaction zones implicit in Reynolds’s rules: a zone of repulsion (short range, strong avoidance), a zone of orientation (intermediate range, velocity matching), and a zone of attraction (long range, approach). Varying the relative radii of these zones produces a phase diagram of qualitatively distinct collective behaviors — a disordered swarm, a rotating torus (milling), and a polarized translating flock. The torus state reproduces circular milling observed in schooling fish and some insects, a pattern absent from Reynolds’s formulation.

A companion study (Couzin et al., 2005) showed that a small informed minority can guide an entire group even when the majority carries no directional information and cannot identify who is informed. The fraction of informed individuals required for reliable guidance decreases with group size, a result with implications for understanding navigation, leadership, and the adaptive value of group living.

Topological interaction in starling murmurations

All models prior to 2008 assumed metric interaction: each individual responds to neighbors within a fixed Euclidean radius \(r\). Ballerini et al. (2008) challenged this assumption using stereoscopic three-dimensional reconstructions of European starling (Sturnus vulgaris) murmurations over Rome. They found that each bird interacts with a fixed number of nearest neighbors — empirically \(k \approx 6\)–\(7\) — regardless of absolute separation. This topological interaction makes the flock robust to density fluctuations: as a predator compresses one wing of a murmuration, interaction distances contract but the interaction network is preserved, maintaining cohesion where a metric rule would cause individuals to lose their neighbors. The finding required revising much prior theory and introduced the stereoscopic field-reconstruction methodology now applied to midges, fish schools, and honeybee swarms.

Statistical mechanics and criticality

Bialek et al. (2012) applied a maximum-entropy statistical mechanics framework to the same starling data. Rather than postulating a mechanistic rule and simulating forward, they inferred the least-biased probability distribution consistent with the observed pairwise velocity correlations. The resulting model has the structure of a ferromagnetic spin system, and it predicts long-range correlations that decay as a power law — not exponentially — across the flock. These scale-free correlations enable a perturbation such as a falcon strike to propagate across the entire group in a time independent of flock size, a property with obvious adaptive value. Critically, the system appears tuned near a critical point where magnetic susceptibility diverges, meaning the flock is maximally sensitive to external perturbation. This critical-tuning hypothesis connects the mechanics of flocking to broader theoretical arguments that natural selection may favor biological systems operating near phase transitions.

Active matter and hydrodynamic theory

The discrete agent models described above have continuum counterparts in active matter physics. Toner & Tu (1995) derived hydrodynamic equations for a flocking fluid by writing down the most general equations consistent with the symmetries of the Vicsek model. Their theory predicts long-range orientational order in two dimensions — ordinarily forbidden by the Mermin-Wagner theorem in equilibrium systems — because active driving breaks time-reversal symmetry in a way that stabilizes the ordered phase. The Toner-Tu equations have since been extended to incorporate growth, confinement, and mixtures of aligning and anti-aligning agents, providing a continuum toolkit applicable to systems ranging from bacterial suspensions to epithelial tissue monolayers.

Recent directions

Current research draws increasingly on large-scale tracking data and machine learning. Katz et al. (2011) used high-speed video of golden shiners to infer pairwise interaction rules from trajectories, finding that fish respond primarily to neighbors’ velocity rather than position — a result more consistent with alignment than attraction. Cavagna et al. (2014) fitted inertial spin-wave models to starling flocks, measuring the effective inertia of collective turns and identifying a propagation speed that is independent of group size. More recent work has deployed graph neural networks to predict collective dynamics from partial observations, and GPU-accelerated simulations now track \(\sim 10^6\) agents at biologically realistic densities, beginning to close the gap between first-principles models and field data at the scales where natural flocks operate.

Behavioral Rules

Each agent (boid) \(i\) carries a position \(\mathbf{p}_i \in \mathbb{R}^2\) and a velocity \(\mathbf{v}_i \in \mathbb{R}^2\). At each discrete timestep the agent perceives its neighborhood

\[N_k(i) \;=\; \bigl\{\, j \neq i \;:\; \|\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j\| < r_k \,\bigr\}\]

and computes three local forces.

Separation

Boids steer to avoid collisions with neighbors that fall within a short-range radius \(r_s\). The repulsive contribution of each neighbor is weighted by the inverse square of the distance, so that very close neighbors exert disproportionately strong avoidance pressure:

\[\mathbf{f}^{\,sep}_i \;=\; \frac{1}{\lvert N_s(i)\rvert} \sum_{j \,\in\, N_s(i)} \frac{\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j}{\|\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j\|^2}\]

Alignment

Boids steer toward the mean heading of neighbors within radius \(r_a\), encouraging local velocity consensus:

\[\mathbf{f}^{\,ali}_i \;=\; \frac{1}{\lvert N_a(i)\rvert} \sum_{j \,\in\, N_a(i)} \mathbf{v}_j\]

Cohesion

Boids steer toward the centroid of neighbors within radius \(r_c\), producing the attractive force that holds the flock together:

\[\mathbf{f}^{\,coh}_i \;=\; \frac{1}{\lvert N_c(i)\rvert} \sum_{j \,\in\, N_c(i)} \mathbf{p}_j \;-\; \mathbf{p}_i\]

Steering and Integration

Each raw force \(\mathbf{f}\) is converted into a steering force by projecting it onto the desired velocity and subtracting the current velocity, then clamping to a maximum force magnitude \(f_{max}\):

\[\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{f}) \;=\; \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{f}}\, v_{max} - \mathbf{v}_i,\; f_{max}\right)\]

where \(\hat{\mathbf{f}} = \mathbf{f}/\|\mathbf{f}\|\) is the unit desired direction and \(v_{max}\) is the maximum speed. The total acceleration is a weighted sum of the three steered forces:

\[\mathbf{a}_i \;=\; w_s\,\mathbf{s}\!\left(\mathbf{f}^{sep}_i\right) \;+\; w_a\,\mathbf{s}\!\left(\mathbf{f}^{ali}_i\right) \;+\; w_c\,\mathbf{s}\!\left(\mathbf{f}^{coh}_i\right)\]

State is updated with a first-order Euler step:

\[\mathbf{v}_i \;\leftarrow\; \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\mathbf{v}_i + \mathbf{a}_i,\; v_{max}\right), \qquad \mathbf{p}_i \;\leftarrow\; \mathbf{p}_i + \mathbf{v}_i\]

The domain is toroidal: agents that exit one edge re-enter from the opposite side.

Simulation Parameters

Parameter Symbol Value
Number of boids \(N\) 30
Maximum speed \(v_{max}\) 2.0 px/frame
Maximum steering force \(f_{max}\) 0.032
Separation radius \(r_s\) 30 px
Alignment radius \(r_a\) 65 px
Cohesion radius \(r_c\) 80 px
Separation weight \(w_s\) 1.7
Alignment weight \(w_a\) 1.1
Cohesion weight \(w_c\) 1.0

The ghost trail is produced by saving a complete positional snapshot of the flock every 10 frames and retaining the most recent 8 snapshots. Each snapshot is rendered at progressively lower opacity (\(\alpha \propto t^2\), where \(t \in (0,1]\) indexes recency), giving a sparse afterimage effect rather than a dense trajectory.

Implementation

The following self-contained JavaScript produces the simulation. It requires only a browser canvas element; no external libraries are needed.

(function () {
  // Attach canvas behind all page content
  var canvas = document.createElement('canvas');
  canvas.style.cssText = 'position:fixed;inset:0;width:100%;height:100%;' +
                         'z-index:0;pointer-events:none;';
  document.body.insertBefore(canvas, document.body.firstChild);
  var ctx = canvas.getContext('2d');

  function resize() {
    canvas.width  = window.innerWidth;
    canvas.height = window.innerHeight;
  }
  resize();
  window.addEventListener('resize', resize);

  /* ── 2-D vector ──────────────────────────────── */
  function Vec(x, y) { this.x = x; this.y = y; }
  Vec.prototype.add   = function(v){ return new Vec(this.x+v.x, this.y+v.y); };
  Vec.prototype.sub   = function(v){ return new Vec(this.x-v.x, this.y-v.y); };
  Vec.prototype.mul   = function(s){ return new Vec(this.x*s,   this.y*s);   };
  Vec.prototype.mag   = function() { return Math.sqrt(this.x*this.x+this.y*this.y)||0.001; };
  Vec.prototype.norm  = function() { var m=this.mag(); return new Vec(this.x/m,this.y/m); };
  Vec.prototype.limit = function(max){
    return this.mag() > max ? this.norm().mul(max) : this;
  };

  /* ── Parameters ──────────────────────────────── */
  var N=30, MAX_SPD=2.0, MAX_F=0.032;
  var R_SEP=30, R_ALI=65, R_COH=80;
  var W_SEP=1.7, W_ALI=1.1, W_COH=1.0;

  /* ── Boid ────────────────────────────────────── */
  function Boid() {
    var W=canvas.width, H=canvas.height;
    this.pos = new Vec(W*0.3+Math.random()*W*0.4, H*0.2+Math.random()*H*0.6);
    var a = Math.random()*Math.PI*2;
    this.vel = new Vec(Math.cos(a)*MAX_SPD, Math.sin(a)*MAX_SPD);
    this.acc = new Vec(0,0);
  }

  Boid.prototype._steer = function(target) {
    return target.norm().mul(MAX_SPD).sub(this.vel).limit(MAX_F);
  };

  Boid.prototype.flock = function(boids) {
    var sep=new Vec(0,0), ali=new Vec(0,0), coh=new Vec(0,0);
    var sc=0, ac=0, cc=0;
    for (var i=0; i<boids.length; i++) {
      if (boids[i]===this) continue;
      var d = this.pos.sub(boids[i].pos).mag();
      if (d < R_SEP){ sep=sep.add(this.pos.sub(boids[i].pos).norm().mul(1/d)); sc++; }
      if (d < R_ALI){ ali=ali.add(boids[i].vel); ac++; }
      if (d < R_COH){ coh=coh.add(boids[i].pos); cc++; }
    }
    var f = new Vec(0,0);
    if (sc) f=f.add(this._steer(sep.mul(1/sc)).mul(W_SEP));
    if (ac) f=f.add(this._steer(ali.mul(1/ac)).mul(W_ALI));
    if (cc) f=f.add(this._steer(coh.mul(1/cc).sub(this.pos)).mul(W_COH));
    this.acc = f;
  };

  Boid.prototype.update = function(boids) {
    this.flock(boids);
    this.vel = this.vel.add(this.acc).limit(MAX_SPD);
    this.pos = this.pos.add(this.vel);
    this.acc = new Vec(0,0);
    // Toroidal wrapping
    var W=canvas.width, H=canvas.height;
    if (this.pos.x < -20)  this.pos.x = W+20;
    if (this.pos.x > W+20) this.pos.x = -20;
    if (this.pos.y < -20)  this.pos.y = H+20;
    if (this.pos.y > H+20) this.pos.y = -20;
  };

  /* ── Rendering ───────────────────────────────── */
  function drawArrow(x, y, angle, alpha, sz) {
    ctx.save();
    ctx.translate(x, y);
    ctx.rotate(angle);
    ctx.strokeStyle = 'rgba(75,65,88,'+alpha.toFixed(3)+')';
    ctx.lineWidth = 1.1;
    ctx.lineCap = 'round';
    ctx.beginPath();
    ctx.moveTo( sz, 0);
    ctx.lineTo(-sz*0.7, -sz*0.55);
    ctx.moveTo( sz, 0);
    ctx.lineTo(-sz*0.7,  sz*0.55);
    ctx.stroke();
    ctx.restore();
  }

  var SNAP_INTERVAL=10, SNAP_COUNT=8, snapshots=[], frame=0;
  var flock = Array.from({length:N}, function(){ return new Boid(); });

  function animate() {
    frame++;
    ctx.clearRect(0, 0, canvas.width, canvas.height);

    for (var i=0; i<flock.length; i++) flock[i].update(flock);

    // Save periodic snapshot for ghost trail
    if (frame % SNAP_INTERVAL === 0) {
      snapshots.push(flock.map(function(b){
        return { x:b.pos.x, y:b.pos.y, a:Math.atan2(b.vel.y,b.vel.x) };
      }));
      if (snapshots.length > SNAP_COUNT) snapshots.shift();
    }

    // Render ghost snapshots (quadratic opacity falloff)
    for (var s=0; s<snapshots.length; s++) {
      var t = (s+1)/(snapshots.length+1);
      var alpha = t*t*0.18;
      for (var j=0; j<snapshots[s].length; j++) {
        var g = snapshots[s][j];
        drawArrow(g.x, g.y, g.a, alpha, 4.5+t*1.5);
      }
    }

    // Render current boids
    for (var i=0; i<flock.length; i++) {
      var b = flock[i];
      drawArrow(b.pos.x, b.pos.y, Math.atan2(b.vel.y,b.vel.x), 0.55, 6);
    }

    requestAnimationFrame(animate);
  }

  animate();
})();
